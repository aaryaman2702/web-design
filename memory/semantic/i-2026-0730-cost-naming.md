# Name the cost, or it is a menu rather than a choice

Observed while building three design treatments. Presented as three strong
options, they read as a pitch with an implied winner — the reader looks for the
one being recommended and picks that.

Adding one line per option stating **what choosing it costs you** changed the
artifact's function entirely. The reader stops looking for the recommendation
and starts weighing.

## Why it works

An option described only by its strengths is not decidable, because nothing
distinguishes the strengths from marketing. The cost is the credibility signal:
it proves the trade-off was actually examined, and it gives the reader the axis
on which to disagree.

It also prevents the worst outcome — someone choosing by inferring which one you
prefer, which produces agreement rather than a decision.

## Where it generalises

Any presentation of alternatives: design directions, architecture proposals,
strategy options, hiring shortlists. This is Constitution V — *trade-offs are
always explicit* — demonstrated as a formatting decision rather than a
principle, which is a useful reduction: the principle is satisfied by adding
one sentence per option.

## Confidence

`medium` — observed once, in one artifact, and its effect was judged by the
author rather than measured. Rises if a reader's response confirms it.
